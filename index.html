Historical Context
Seoul · Joseon Dynasty & Japanese Colonial Era
Gyeongbokgung Palace was built in 1395 by King Taejo, founder of the Joseon Dynasty — a Confucian state that ruled Korea for 500 years. The Joseon court produced extraordinary art, literature, and the Korean hangul alphabet (invented by King Sejong in 1443, one of history's most deliberate and successful acts of cultural empowerment). The palace was burned during Toyotomi Hideyoshi's invasion of Korea (Imjin War, 1592–1598) — a seven-year campaign that killed hundreds of thousands and removed much of Korea's cultural heritage, including craftspeople and artisans forcibly taken to Japan. Ironically, this invasion triggered a golden age of Japanese ceramics — Korean potters brought to Japan founded the great Arita and Karatsu traditions.

In 1910, Japan formally annexed Korea. The colonial government demolished 90% of Gyeongbokgung and built the Japanese Government-General building directly in front of the remaining hall — a deliberate act of symbolic domination. The building was torn down in 1995. The palace restoration you see today is ongoing and deeply political — it is Korea reclaiming its own story. The changing of the guard ceremony is not tourist theatre — it is cultural reclamation performed daily.

The colonial period (1910–1945) suppressed Korean language, culture and identity systematically. Understanding this before arriving in Japan — the coloniser — makes everything that follows more complex and more interesting. The strained contemporary relationship between Korea and Japan, the disputes over historical acknowledgment, the comfort women memorials you may see in Seoul — all are threads of this unfinished history.

Local Food & Specialities
Seoul · What to Eat
Samgyetang — ginseng chicken soup
A whole young chicken stuffed with glutinous rice, ginseng, jujube and garlic, simmered for hours in a clear broth. Eaten on the hottest days of the Korean calendar (sambok — which falls in July) as 'fight heat with heat.' Deeply restorative. Best at Tosokchon near Gyeongbokgung — arrive before 11:30am or queue. ₩18,000.

Bibim naengmyeon — cold buckwheat noodles
The essential July dish — buckwheat noodles served ice-cold with gochujang, cucumber, pear, boiled egg and thin beef, mixed vigorously at the table. Refreshing, invigorating, deeply Korean. Woo Lae Oak (est. 1946) is the institution — ₩15,000.

Hanwoo at Majang Market
Korea's native beef, comparable to wagyu. Select your cut (1++ grade is highest) at a butcher downstairs at Majang Meat Market, pay ₩6,000pp grilling fee upstairs, cook over charcoal at the table. 20–30% cheaper than a restaurant. Ask for chadolbaegi (paper-thin brisket, cooks in seconds), galbi (short rib) or deungsim (sirloin).

Makgeolli and pajeon in Ikseon-dong
Milky, slightly fizzy rice wine (6–8% ABV), served in a kettle and poured into shallow bowls. Pairs perfectly with haemul pajeon — crispy seafood pancake with squid and spring onion. The best craft makgeolli bars are in Ikseon-dong — try regional varieties from different provinces. ₩5,000–8,000 a jug.

Gwangjang Market
Bindaetteok (mung bean pancakes, fried on enormous griddles), yukhoe (raw beef tartare with pear and egg yolk — contains pine nuts, easy to avoid), mayak kimbap (tiny addictive rice rolls). Go before 11am before tour groups arrive. Gwangjang Market.

Oegojip Seoulleongtang
A Seoul institution — milky ox bone soup (seolleongtang), simmered for 24 hours until white, served with thin noodles and sliced beef. Season yourself at the table with salt and spring onions. Open 24 hours. Quintessential Seoul comfort food at ₩12,000.

⚠️ Nut allergy: Korean food is very safe for tree nuts and peanuts. Pine nuts appear in yukhoe (raw beef tartare) — ask them to omit. Sesame oil and seeds everywhere but fine for you.

Historical Context
Tokyo · Edo, the Shogunate & Meiji Restoration
Tokyo was not Japan's capital until 1869. For 1,075 years the emperor lived in Kyoto. The city now called Tokyo was Edo — a fishing village that Tokugawa Ieyasu chose as his base in 1603 after winning the Battle of Sekigahara and becoming Shogun. Under the Tokugawa shogunate, Edo grew into the world's largest city by 1700 with over one million inhabitants. The shogunate practised sakoku — near-total national isolation — for 250 years. No Japanese could leave on pain of death, no foreigner could enter except Dutch merchants confined to a tiny island in Nagasaki. This isolation created the distinctly Japanese culture you experience everywhere: the aesthetics, the social rituals, the food traditions, the crafts.

The sakoku ended in 1853 when American Commodore Matthew Perry sailed four warships into Edo Bay and demanded Japan open for trade. The resulting humiliation triggered civil war — the Boshin War — and the Meiji Restoration of 1868, which ended the shogunate and began Japan's extraordinary modernisation. The emperor moved from Kyoto to Edo, renamed it Tokyo, and Japan transformed from a feudal society into an industrial world power in 40 years — one of history's most remarkable accelerations.

Yanaka is one of the few districts that survived the 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake (140,000 dead) and the 1945 American firebombing raids (destroing 50% of Tokyo). Walking its streets is genuinely walking Edo. Senso-ji was founded in 645 AD — predating the city around it by over a thousand years.

Historical Context
Hokkaido · The Ainu & the Meiji Frontier
Hokkaido is Japan's youngest territory — a frontier, not an ancient land. The island has been inhabited for 15,000 years by the Ainu people — Japan's indigenous population, with their own language, spiritual traditions, bear ceremonies and ecological knowledge entirely distinct from mainland Japanese culture. The Ainu called the island Ainu Mosir, 'The Quiet Land of Humans.'

In 1869, the Meiji government renamed the island Hokkaido and launched systematic colonisation (kaitaku) modelled on the American frontier settlement. The Ainu were dispossessed of their lands, their language was banned in schools, and their population — estimated at 70,000 before contact — collapsed through disease and forced assimilation. A formal government apology was not issued until 2019. The settlements you drive through — Sapporo, Otaru, Yoichi — are all under 150 years old. Otaru's canal district was built by Meiji-era merchants in the 1890s.

The Nikka Whisky distillery in Yoichi was founded in 1934 by Masataka Taketsuru, who had studied distilling in Scotland and returned to recreate the cold, misty Highland climate he found in Hokkaido. The whisky he made now outscores its Scottish originals at international competition. Hokkaido's food culture — dairy, lamb, corn, cold-water seafood — reflects its settler-colonial history and agricultural systems deliberately imported from Europe and America.

Historical Context
Miyakojima · The Ryukyu Kingdom & the Pacific War
The islands of Okinawa, including Miyakojima, were not part of Japan until 1879. For 450 years (1429–1879) they formed the Ryukyu Kingdom — an independent maritime trading nation that sat at the crossroads of China, Japan, Korea and Southeast Asia. Ryukyuan merchants traded Chinese silk for Japanese silver, Southeast Asian spices for Korean ceramics. The kingdom was so diplomatically skilled that it maintained tributary relationships with China while nominally under Japanese influence — playing both powers against each other with extraordinary finesse for centuries.

In 1609, the Satsuma domain of southern Japan invaded Ryukyu and made it a vassal state while allowing it to continue its China trade (useful to Japan, which was under sakoku). In 1879, the Meiji government formally annexed the islands and abolished the Ryukyu Kingdom, imprisoning the last king. The Okinawan language was banned. The cultural suppression was so thorough that many aspects of Ryukyuan culture — the textiles, the music, the food, the language — survived only in rural areas and in the memories of elders.

The Battle of Okinawa (April–June 1945) was among the bloodiest of the Pacific War. An estimated 110,000 Japanese soldiers, 12,000 American soldiers and 94,000–150,000 Okinawan civilians died — approximately a third of the entire civilian population. The island was captured and administered by the United States from 1945 until 1972. The American military presence on Okinawa (US bases still occupy 18% of the main island) remains a source of significant political tension. Miyakojima's relative distance from the main island sheltered it from some of the war's worst destruction but not its broader trauma. Understanding Okinawa as a colonised, then occupied, then returned territory — one that is distinct from mainland Japan in culture, language and history — is essential context for your visit.

Miyakojima · What to Eat
Soki Soba — Okinawan noodle soup
Nothing like mainland Japanese soba. The noodles are made from wheat (not buckwheat), flat, soft and slightly chewy. The broth is a light but complex combination of pork and katsuobushi. The topping is soki — braised pork spare rib, soft enough to fall from the bone, with a sweetness from the simmering liquid. Served with tart pickled ginger and a splash of shikuwasa citrus. ¥700–1,200. The most distinctively Okinawan dish and the one to eat first on arrival.

Goya Champuru — bitter melon stir-fry
The iconic Okinawan dish — bitter melon (goya) stir-fried with tofu, egg and SPAM (introduced during the American occupation, now fully integrated into Okinawan cuisine). The bitterness of the goya is the point — Okinawans consider it a health food responsible partly for the island's extraordinary longevity statistics. Approach it with curiosity: it is genuinely good once you stop expecting it to be sweet. ¥800–1,200.

燦伊良部島店 · Irabu Island
Rated 4.7 stars, ¥1,000–2,000. Cross the Irabu Bridge (the longest toll-free bridge in Japan, 3.5km) to Irabu Island and find this local restaurant serving fresh seafood and Okinawan dishes. Order whatever the chef recommends that day — the coral reef around Miyakojima produces grouper, snapper and trevally of exceptional quality. The rice is local Miyako rice, slightly different in texture from mainland Japanese rice.

Awamori — Okinawa's indigenous spirit
Distilled from Thai indica rice using black koji mould — different from all mainland Japanese spirits. Aged in clay pots (kame); older awamori (kusu, meaning 'old sake') develops extraordinary nutty, earthy depth. Try it on the rocks (on za rokku) with a water chaser. 25–43% ABV. A bottle of aged kusu from Miyakojima makes the most meaningful souvenir of the Okinawa portion of the trip — ask at any local liquor shop for their best aged bottle.

Fresh grilled fish and kaisendon
Any waterfront restaurant in Hirara (Miyakojima's main town) serves the morning's catch — grouper, trevally, snapper, octopus. A kaisendon (sashimi rice bowl) using local fish costs ¥1,500–2,500 and is among the best versions of this dish you will find anywhere in Japan.

Tropical fruit shaved ice (kakigori)
Essential in July's Okinawan heat — ice shaved to a snow-like consistency (not crushed), drenched in condensed milk and local fruit syrups: shikuwasa (a small citrus unique to Okinawa, intensely aromatic), passion fruit, mango. ¥500–800. Find it at any beach kiosk or cafe near the water.

⚠️ IMPORTANT — Jimami Tofu
Jimami tofu (じーまーみ豆腐) looks like tofu but is made entirely from peanuts. It is a Miyakojima/Okinawan speciality and may be served as a starter in set meals without obvious labelling. Always ask before eating anything described as tofu in Okinawa. Say: 'Kore ni pinatsu wa haitte imasu ka?' (これにピーナッツは入っていますか？) — 'Does this contain peanuts?'

Kansai · Imperial Kyoto, Buddhist Koyasan & Merchant Osaka
In 794 AD, Emperor Kanmu moved the imperial capital to a new city built on a grid plan modelled on the Tang Dynasty capital of Chang'an (modern Xi'an). He named it Heian-kyo — 'Capital of Peace and Tranquility.' It remained Japan's imperial capital for 1,075 years, until Emperor Meiji moved to Tokyo in 1869. It is now called Kyoto.

The Heian period (794–1185) produced what many historians consider the apex of Japanese court culture. Lady Murasaki Shikibu wrote The Tale of Genji at the Heian court around 1008 — the world's first novel, a 1,000-page psychological portrait of aristocratic life written by a woman in a court that considered Chinese the only serious literary language. The court aesthetic of mono no aware ('the pathos of things,' a sensitivity to impermanence) and wabi-sabi (beauty in transience and imperfection) emerged from this period and still governs Japanese aesthetics today.

Koyasan was founded in 816 AD by the monk Kobo Daishi (Kukai, 774–835), who had studied esoteric Buddhism in Tang China and returned to create Shingon Buddhism — a synthesis of Buddhist teaching and Japanese spiritual sensibility. He is said to be not dead but in eternal meditation in his mausoleum at Okunoin — every morning monks bring him breakfast, which has been delivered for 1,200 years without interruption.

The Gion Matsuri began in 869 AD as a ritual to appease the gods during a devastating plague — the same function that religious festivals have served since the first cities. The 23 enormous floats (yamaboko) took craftsmen a year to build and used textiles from China, Persia and Belgium brought through the Silk Road trade. The same floats are assembled each year using the same woodworking techniques, the same textiles preserved for centuries. You will watch this festival on 18 July — a living tradition 1,157 years old.

Osaka was the commercial heart of Japan during the Edo period — the city of merchants while Kyoto was the city of the emperor and Edo was the city of the shogun. The Osaka proverb kuidaore ('eat until you drop') reflects this merchant culture: wealth expressed through eating well rather than aristocratic refinement. The contrast between Osaka's exuberant street food culture and Kyoto's restrained kaiseki is a contrast between merchant Japan and court Japan that you will taste directly on this trip.

Historical Context
Hiroshima -- 8:15am, 6 August 1945
At 8:15am on 6 August 1945, the US bomber Enola Gay dropped an atomic bomb called Little Boy over central Hiroshima. The explosion killed 70,000 to 80,000 people instantly. By the end of 1945 the death toll had reached up to 166,000 from blast, fire and radiation. Three days later a second bomb fell on Nagasaki. Japan surrendered on 15 August 1945.

The decision to use nuclear weapons on civilian populations remains the most contested moral decision of the 20th century. The Peace Memorial Museum does not make this argument for you. It presents the human experience through the belongings of individuals and asks you to draw your own conclusions. It is among the most important museum visits you will make in your lives.

The A-Bomb Dome (Genbaku Dome) stood directly below the hypocentre. The bomb detonated 600m above it -- the near-vertical downward force preserved the dome's skeleton while destroying everything around it for kilometres. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1996.

Miyajima's Itsukushima Shrine was first built in the 6th century, expanded by warlord Taira no Kiyomori in 1168. The decision to build the shrine over the water so that pilgrims felt they were entering a floating sacred world is one of the great acts of religious design in history. For centuries, no one could be born or die on the island.

Historical Context
Tokyo II -- Asakusa, Senso-ji and the Post-War Miracle
Senso-ji Temple was founded in 645 AD, making it Tokyo's oldest temple -- predating the city around it by over a thousand years. According to tradition, two fishermen brothers pulled a golden image of Kannon (the Buddhist goddess of mercy) from the Sumida River. The image has been enshrined here ever since, surviving the Warring States period, the 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake and the 1945 American firebombing raids. The current temple was rebuilt in 1958. Arrive before 7am to find the sanctuary nearly empty.

Asakusa was the entertainment district of Edo -- kabuki theatres and teahouses for the merchant class. The Nakamise Shopping Street dates from the Edo period, when shopkeepers were granted the right to sell goods to pilgrims -- one of the world's oldest continuously operating commercial streets.

The post-war economic miracle transformed Tokyo from a city of ashes in 1945 into the world's largest metropolitan economy by 1980. Japan's GDP grew at 10% annually for 20 years -- unprecedented in human history. The country rebuilt under its pacifist constitution, renounced war as state policy, and channelled energy into manufacturing and technology. Toyota, Sony, Honda and Panasonic are all products of this 40-year transformation.

The Ryogoku Kokugikan sits in the sumo district where wrestlers' stables have been concentrated since the Edo period. Sumo's origins are in Shinto ritual -- originally performed at shrines as communication with the gods. The pre-bout rituals (foot-stamping, salt-throwing, prolonged staring) are Shinto ceremony as much as sport.

History Guide

Japan & Korea · From Ancient Times to the Present Day
Your itinerary passes through 14 centuries of Japanese history and a thousand years of Korean history in 23 days. Taken together, the places you visit tell one of the great stories of human civilisation. Each chapter below is collapsible. Read them on the plane, or open them as you arrive in each city.
14,000 BC – 794 AD
The Ancient World
Jomon hunter-gatherers, Yayoi rice farmers, the imperial family emerges, Buddhism arrives, the Nara capital.
▿
The Jomon People (14,000 - 300 BC)
Japan has been inhabited for at least 30,000 years. The Jomon people are among the world's first known potters, producing fired ceramics as early as 14,000 BC. They lived as hunter-gatherers in extraordinary abundance: the Japan Sea provided salmon, tuna and shellfish; the forests provided deer, boar and chestnuts. Around 300 BC, waves of migrants from the Korean peninsula brought wet-rice agriculture, bronze-working and iron tools. The Yayoi people intermarried with and gradually displaced the Jomon, transforming Japan from a forested subsistence culture into a rice-farming society capable of generating agricultural surplus -- and therefore, hierarchy, warfare, and eventually cities.

The Kofun Period and the Imperial Family (300 - 538 AD)
Named for the enormous burial mounds (kofun) built for the ruling elite -- keyhole-shaped earthworks up to 486 metres long, larger than the Great Pyramid of Giza. The largest, Daisen Kofun near Osaka, remains the largest burial mound in the world by area. The imperial family whose lineage continues to Emperor Naruhito today emerged from the Yamato clan of this period -- making the Japanese imperial line the world's oldest continuous hereditary monarchy, stretching unbroken from the 4th century AD to the present day.

Buddhism Arrives (552 AD)
Buddhism arrived from Korea in 552 AD as a diplomatic gift, along with sutras, a bronze Buddha image and a recommendation that Japan adopt the new religion. The court was immediately split -- the Soga clan favoured adoption, the Mononobe and Nakatomi clans opposed it as a threat to Shinto. Civil war followed. The Soga won. But crucially, Buddhism was not adopted instead of Shinto -- it was added to it. The synthesis still defines Japanese religious life: Shinto shrines for births and New Year, Buddhist temples for funerals and the afterlife.

Prince Shotoku (574-622 AD) drafted Japan's first constitution, sent diplomatic missions to Tang China, and built Horyu-ji Temple -- the world's oldest surviving wooden structure, still standing today.

The Nara Period (710 - 794 AD)
Japan's first permanent capital was built at Nara in 710 AD -- a grid-plan city of 200,000 people modelled on Tang Dynasty Chang'an. The Todai-ji Temple was built in 752 AD, housing Japan's largest bronze Buddha at 15 metres tall, cast in a national effort requiring most of Japan's copper and gold. The project nearly bankrupted the state but announced Japan's arrival as a sophisticated Buddhist civilisation. The capital moved from Nara to Kyoto in 794 AD partly to escape the growing political power of Buddhist monasteries, which had accumulated land, wealth and private armies.
794 – 1185
The Heian Period
Imperial Kyoto, The Tale of Genji, the invention of Japanese literature by court women, the rise of the samurai, the Mongol invasions.
▿
The Heian Capital (794 - 1185)
Emperor Kanmu built Heian-kyo ("Capital of Peace and Tranquility") in 794 AD. What followed was Japan's golden age of court culture -- 400 years in which a small aristocratic elite produced art, literature and aesthetic philosophy of extraordinary refinement.

The court aesthetic was built around mono no aware -- "the pathos of things," a bittersweet sensitivity to the transience of beauty. Cherry blossoms were perfect because they fell. Autumn leaves were beautiful because they died. This is not pessimism but deep attentiveness: impermanence makes the present moment precious. This sensitivity still governs Japanese aesthetics today -- in kaiseki cuisine's insistence on seasonal ingredients, in the Japanese appreciation of wabi-sabi (beauty in imperfection and age).

The Literature of the Heian Court
The court's most remarkable cultural achievements were produced by women. The men wrote in classical Chinese. The women wrote in the newly developed hiragana syllabic alphabet -- considered too informal for serious use -- and in doing so created Japanese literature. Lady Murasaki Shikibu wrote The Tale of Genji around 1008 AD -- the world's first psychological novel, 54 chapters of extraordinary subtlety. Her contemporary Sei Shonagon wrote The Pillow Book -- observations of remarkable wit and precision. Both were ladies-in-waiting. Both wrote in a script men refused to use. Both created the foundation of Japanese literary tradition.

The Rise of the Samurai and the Mongol Invasions
While the court pursued aesthetics, a warrior class was rising in the provinces. The samurai synthesised Buddhist impermanence (the warrior accepts death), Zen directness (action over deliberation) and Confucian loyalty (absolute devotion to one's lord) into what became bushido -- "the way of the warrior." In 1274 and 1281, Kublai Khan launched massive invasion fleets. Both were destroyed by typhoons the Japanese called kamikaze -- "divine wind." The word, later grotesquely repurposed for WWII suicide pilots, originally meant divine deliverance.

Connections to your itinerary: The Itsukushima Shrine on Miyajima was expanded by Taira no Kiyomori in 1168 at the peak of his power -- one year before the Genpei War destroyed his clan. The Gion Matsuri began in 869 AD as a plague-prevention ritual -- a living Heian-period institution now in its 1,157th year.
1185 – 1603
The Feudal Era
Kamakura shogunate, Zen Buddhism, 100 years of civil war, Nobunaga, Hideyoshi, Tokugawa. The world you will see was built here.
▿
The Kamakura Shogunate and Zen Buddhism (1185 - 1336)
Minamoto no Yoritomo won the Genpei War and established Japan's first military government (bakufu) at Kamakura. The emperor continued to reign in Kyoto but the shogun governed. This duality -- an imperial family with divine legitimacy and no power, a military government with power and no divinity -- defined Japanese politics for 700 years. Zen Buddhism arrived from China during the Kamakura period and found its natural audience among the samurai. Its emphasis on direct experience, discipline and simplicity resonated with a warrior class that needed a philosophy of death. The tea ceremony, Zen rock garden, Noh theatre and minimalist architectural aesthetic you will encounter everywhere in Japan all emerged from this Zen-samurai synthesis.

Oda Nobunaga (1534 - 1582)
The first of the three great unifiers. A tactical revolutionary: the first Japanese commander to use firearms (acquired from Portuguese traders who arrived in 1543) in massed formations. He was utterly ruthless -- in 1571 he burned the Buddhist monastery complex of Enryakuji on Mount Hiei, killing thousands of monks, women and children. He welcomed Jesuit missionaries partly as a counter to Buddhist power. In 1582, his general Akechi Mitsuhide turned against him. Surrounded at Honno-ji temple in Kyoto, Nobunaga performed seppuku rather than surrender.

Toyotomi Hideyoshi (1537 - 1598)
Born a peasant, Hideyoshi became the most powerful man in Japan by the age of 40 purely through ability. He completed unification, then made it permanent through the "sword hunt" of 1588 which confiscated all weapons from non-samurai -- permanently separating the warrior class from the farming class. He then launched two catastrophic invasions of Korea (1592-1598) which killed approximately one million Koreans and failed completely. The cultural consequences for Japan were transformative: Korean potters forcibly taken to Japan founded the great ceramic traditions of Arita, Karatsu and Satsuma. The Senjokaku Pavilion on Miyajima was commissioned by Hideyoshi in 1587 for soldiers killed in his Korean campaigns. He died in 1598 before it was completed. It has remained unfinished ever since.

Tokugawa Ieyasu (1543 - 1616)
The most patient of the three unifiers. At the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600 -- 160,000 soldiers on a mountain pass in fog -- Ieyasu's eastern coalition defeated the western alliance. He became Shogun in 1603 and founded a dynasty that ruled Japan for 265 years. His masterstroke was institutional: the sankin-kotai system required all domain lords to spend alternate years in Edo and their home domains, leaving their families in Edo as permanent hostages. This kept potential rivals poor and controllable. It also created the great highway system of Japan, the post-towns, and the street food culture -- all built to service the daimyo processions.
1603 – 1868
The Edo Period
265 years of isolation. The world's largest city. Kabuki, Hokusai, Basho. Perry's Black Ships end it all.
▿
Sakoku -- The Closed Country (1633 - 1853)
The Tokugawa shogunate's policy of sakoku ("chained country") was triggered by Christianity: by 1600 Japan had 300,000 Christian converts, creating a parallel loyalty to Rome that threatened the social order. The Shimabara Rebellion of 1637-38 -- a peasant uprising with Christian leadership, suppressed with 37,000 killed -- provided the final justification for expelling all missionaries and closing Japan. No Japanese could leave on pain of death. No foreigner could enter except Dutch merchants confined to Dejima -- a fan-shaped artificial island in Nagasaki harbour, connected to the mainland by a single guarded bridge. Through this tiny window, Japan absorbed Western science (astronomy, medicine, gunnery) in a body of scholarship called Rangaku -- "Dutch learning" -- that proved crucial to later modernisation.

The World's Largest City
Edo grew to over one million inhabitants by 1700 -- larger than London, Paris or Beijing. The city was built around the shogun's castle in concentric rings: samurai districts surrounding the castle, then merchant and artisan districts, then the licensed pleasure quarters. The Yoshiwara -- the great licensed brothel district -- was an economic and cultural institution that produced its own fashions, language and arts. The geisha culture that survives today in Kyoto's Gion district developed partly in response to the more transactional culture of the Yoshiwara.

The Arts of the Floating World
The Edo period's most enduring cultural products came from the merchant class -- officially the lowest of the four social ranks yet in practice the wealthiest. They funded kabuki theatre (scandalous, spectacular, performed by male actors in female roles after women were banned from the stage in 1629), ukiyo-e woodblock prints (Hokusai's Great Wave of Kanagawa was one of a series of 36 views of Mount Fuji, produced when Hokusai was 71, sold for the price of a bowl of noodles), and the haiku tradition of Matsuo Basho whose Narrow Road to the Deep North (1689) is one of the great travel narratives of world literature.

The tea ceremony, systematised by Sen no Rikyu in the 16th century, became the central performance of Japanese elite culture -- a ritual requiring years of study whose philosophy of wabi-sabi (treating rough, asymmetric, aged objects as more beautiful than perfect expensive ones) still governs Japanese aesthetics and the kaiseki cuisine you will experience.

The End of Isolation
On 8 July 1853, Commodore Matthew Perry sailed four warships into Edo Bay -- steam-powered, their black funnels giving them the name "Black Ships" (kurofune). Japan had seen no foreign military technology for 250 years. Perry delivered a letter from President Fillmore demanding Japan open its ports. He returned in 1854 with eight ships and Japan signed the Convention of Kanagawa. The humiliation was existential: Japan had maintained isolation for 250 years and in a single encounter had been revealed as militarily helpless against a mid-ranking Western naval power. The shogunate's inability to respond effectively destroyed its authority. Within 15 years it had fallen.

Connections: Yanaka and Asakusa are fragments of Edo-period Tokyo that survived the 1923 earthquake and 1945 firebombing. Walking them is walking the Edo city.
1868 – 1912
The Meiji Restoration
Japan remakes itself in 40 years. The samurai abolished. The Iwakura Mission. Asia's first industrial power. Korea annexed.
▿
The Meiji Restoration (1868)
A coalition of samurai clans from Satsuma and Choshu toppled the Tokugawa shogunate in the Boshin War (1868-69), rallying around the young Emperor Meiji (15 years old at restoration). The government's programme was captured in their first charter oath: "Knowledge shall be sought throughout the world." The Iwakura Mission of 1871-73 sent 48 senior officials to spend 18 months studying Western governance, legal systems, military organisation, schools, hospitals, railways and factories. They returned and rebuilt Japan -- a new civil law code (based on French and German models), a conscript army (Prussian model), a navy (British Royal Navy model), universities, railways, a modern currency, compulsory education.

The Abolition of the Samurai
The samurai class -- whose hereditary right to carry swords and receive stipends had defined Japanese society for 700 years -- was abolished by edict. The Satsuma Rebellion of 1877, led by Saigo Takamori (the greatest samurai general of the restoration, who had himself helped abolish the samurai class), was defeated by a conscript army of peasants using modern rifles. Saigo died by seppuku. The samurai era ended. The Last Samurai is a fictionalised account of these events -- the historical core is real.

State Shinto and the Nationalist Project
The Meiji government elevated the emperor to divine status and made Shinto the de facto state religion -- creating "State Shinto" as a nationalist ideology. Meiji Shrine in Tokyo, which you will visit, was built in 1920 to enshrine Emperor Meiji's spirit. The shrine's forest (70,000 trees donated from across Japan) was deliberately designed to feel timeless and ancient, though it is barely a century old.

Japan as Imperial Power
By 1895 Japan had defeated China (taking Taiwan). By 1905 it had defeated Russia -- the first Asian nation to defeat a European great power in modern warfare, a shock to global assumptions of racial hierarchy. Korea became a Japanese protectorate in 1905 and was formally annexed in 1910. Gyeongbokgung Palace -- which you will visit -- was systematically demolished as an act of colonial cultural erasure.
1912 – 1945
Empire and War
Brief democracy, military government, the Nanjing Massacre, Pearl Harbor, Hiroshima. The catastrophic arc of Japanese imperialism.
▿
The Taisho Democracy (1912 - 1926)
The brief Taisho period was Japan's democratic moment -- multi-party government, a relatively free press, jazz cafes, cinema, women's suffrage movements. The 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake (7.9 magnitude, 140,000 dead, half of Tokyo destroyed) ended this moment brutally. In the chaos, Korean residents in Tokyo were massacred by mobs acting on false rumours -- an estimated 6,000 Koreans killed in three days, with police complicity.

Military Government and War in China
Through the 1920s-30s, the military gradually captured control of the Japanese state through political assassination and constitutional structures that gave the military direct access to the emperor. Full-scale war with China began in July 1937. In December 1937, Japanese forces captured Nanjing (Nanking) -- the Nanjing Massacre killed an estimated 200,000-300,000 civilians and prisoners of war over six weeks. The event remains deeply contested in Japan and is a key source of contemporary China-Japan tension.

Pearl Harbor and the Pacific War (1941 - 1945)
Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941 was a calculated gamble: Admiral Yamamoto knew the US would eventually defeat Japan but believed a devastating first strike might force a negotiated peace before American industrial capacity fully mobilised. Japanese forces achieved stunning early victories -- Malaya, Singapore, the Philippines, Burma, the Dutch East Indies fell within months. The turning point was the Battle of Midway (June 1942): the US destroyed four Japanese aircraft carriers and their trained pilots in three days, permanently shifting the balance of naval power.

Hiroshima and the End (1945)
By early 1945 Japan was being systematically destroyed from the air. The firebombing of Tokyo on 9-10 March 1945 killed an estimated 80,000-100,000 civilians in a single night. The atomic bomb dropped on Hiroshima on 6 August 1945 killed 70,000-80,000 instantly, with total deaths reaching 90,000-166,000 by year end. Nagasaki followed three days later. Japan surrendered on 15 August 1945.

The Battle of Okinawa (April-June 1945) killed approximately a third of the entire civilian population of Okinawa -- including the islands around Miyakojima. The island was administered by the United States until 1972. US military bases still cover 18% of the main island.
1945 – Present
The Post-War Miracle
MacArthur's occupation, the pacifist constitution, GDP at 10% for 20 years, the economic bubble, the lost decades, contemporary Japan.
▿
The American Occupation (1945 - 1952)
General MacArthur arrived in Tokyo on 30 August 1945 and governed Japan as Supreme Commander for seven years. The occupation's achievements were extraordinary: a new pacifist constitution (Article 9 permanently renounces war as an instrument of state policy, still in force today), land reform that broke up feudal landholding, the dissolution of the zaibatsu (great industrial-financial combines that had funded militarism), women's suffrage, and a free press. The constitution was written in six days by a team of American lawyers and military officers in February 1946, translated into Japanese, and adopted largely unchanged.

The Economic Miracle (1952 - 1973)
Japan's economic recovery was the most rapid in world history. The Korean War (1950-53) provided the initial stimulus through US military procurement orders. The government channelled recovery through MITI (Ministry of International Trade and Industry), which allocated foreign currency, directed investment, and protected domestic industries while forcing them to become globally competitive. Toyota developed the Toyota Production System (just-in-time manufacturing, zero inventory, continuous improvement) that became the global standard. Sony's transistor radio (1955) was Japan's first major consumer electronics export. Honda grew from a bicycle engine attachment in 1948 to the world's largest motorcycle manufacturer by 1960. GDP grew at 10% annually for 20 years -- unprecedented in human history. The 1964 Tokyo Olympics marked Japan's symbolic return to the world stage: on 1 October 1964 -- the day the Games opened -- the Tokaido Shinkansen began operating, the world's first high-speed railway.

The Bubble and the Lost Decades
The Plaza Accord of 1985 caused the yen to appreciate dramatically, making Japanese exports expensive. Companies awash with cheap credit invested in domestic real estate and stocks instead. At the peak (1989), the land under the Imperial Palace in Tokyo was theoretically worth more than all the real estate in California. The bubble burst in 1990. The 1990s are called the Lost Decade; by some measures it became two.

Contemporary Japan (2000 - Present)
Japan in 2026 navigates multiple pressures: a shrinking, ageing population (birth rate 1.2, among the world's lowest); deflation and stagnation persisting for 30 years; a pacifist constitution under pressure as China's military power grows; unresolved historical grievances with Korea and China; the Okinawa base controversy; and the question of Japan's 21st-century role. Against these pressures, Japan maintains extraordinary cultural coherence and daily-life quality: among the world's safest countries, outstanding public infrastructure, deep craftsmanship traditions extending from pre-industrial Japan to the present, and a food culture of global influence.

Your trip as a history lesson: Seoul (Joseon Dynasty, Japanese colonialism, Korean resilience) -- Tokyo (Edo merchants and Meiji modernisation) -- Hokkaido (Ainu dispossession and frontier settlement) -- Miyakojima (Ryukyu Kingdom and Pacific War) -- Koyasan (1,200 years of Shingon Buddhism) -- Kyoto (1,075 years as imperial capital) -- Hiroshima (the pivot of the modern world, 8:15am 6 August 1945) -- Tokyo again (Senso-ji since 645 AD, post-war economic miracle). Few itineraries of this length tell so complete a historical story.
1392 – Present
Korea · History & the Japan Relationship
Joseon Dynasty, the Imjin War, Japanese colonialism, the Korean War, the Han River economic miracle, K-pop and contemporary Korea.
▿
A 1,400-Year Relationship
The relationship between Korea and Japan is among the most historically complex bilateral relationships in the world. Buddhism, writing, Confucianism, advanced metalworking and agricultural techniques all reached Japan from or through the Korean peninsula between 300-600 AD. Several members of the Japanese imperial family have acknowledged Korean ancestry.

The Joseon Dynasty (1392 - 1897)
Founded by General Yi Seonggye in 1392 after overthrowing the Goryeo kingdom, Joseon was Asia's longest-ruling Confucian dynasty -- 500 years built on Neo-Confucian philosophy and an examination-based meritocracy. Its greatest achievement was the hangul alphabet in 1443 by King Sejong -- one of history's most deliberate and rational writing systems, whose letter shapes encode the position of tongue and lips in pronouncing each sound. The late Joseon period saw factional politics, peasant uprisings and growing external pressure.

The Imjin War (1592 - 1598)
Toyotomi Hideyoshi invaded Korea with 160,000 troops in 1592 -- the largest overseas Japanese military operation in history to that date. Admiral Yi Sun-sin -- whose turtle ships (geobukseon), possibly the world's first ironclad warships, repeatedly destroyed Japanese supply fleets -- and Ming Dynasty Chinese forces eventually halted the invasion. An estimated one million Koreans died. Yi Sun-sin was killed in the final battle and remains Korea's greatest military hero. His statue at Gwanghwamun Square faces south, toward Japan. The cultural consequence for Japan was profound: Korean potters forcibly taken founded the great ceramic traditions of Arita, Karatsu and Satsuma.

The Opening of Korea and Annexation (1876 - 1910)
Japan used gunboat diplomacy on Korea in 1876 -- the same method Perry had used on Japan in 1853 -- to force the Ganghwa Treaty opening Korean ports to Japanese trade. Korea declared the Korean Empire in 1897 as a final attempt at sovereign modernisation, but Japan became the protectorate in 1905 (after defeating Russia) and formally annexed Korea in 1910.

The Colonial Period (1910 - 1945)
Japan administered Korea for 35 years. Korean language was banned in schools, Korean names replaced with Japanese names under the 1940 name-change order, Korean land expropriated, hundreds of thousands mobilised as forced labour. The comfort women system remains the most emotionally charged unresolved grievance between the two countries. The Gyeongbokgung Palace you will visit was demolished to 10% of its original structure; a massive Japanese Government-General building was erected in front of the surviving hall -- torn down in 1995. The ongoing restoration is an act of national healing.

Liberation, Division and the Korean War (1945 - 1953)
Korea was liberated in 1945 then immediately divided between Soviet (north) and American (south) occupation zones. War began on 25 June 1950, killed an estimated 3 million people, and ended in a ceasefire technically still in force. South Korea's GDP per capita in 1953 was lower than sub-Saharan Africa.

The South Korean Economic Miracle (1961 - 1997)
General Park Chung-hee's authoritarian development model -- state-directed capitalism channelled through family-owned conglomerates called chaebols -- produced GDP growth averaging 8-10% annually for three decades. Samsung (founded 1938 as a grocery company, now the world's largest memory chip maker), Hyundai (construction in 1947, now a top-five global car manufacturer), LG, Lotte, SK -- all products of this period. GDP per capita rose from $79 in 1960 to $33,000 today. The transformation of Seoul from bombed-out capital in 1953 to one of the world's great cities -- the Han River Miracle -- is visible everywhere: Bukchon's old hanok streets coexist with a gleaming modern city built within a single human lifetime.

Democratisation, 1988 Olympics and Contemporary Korea
Mass protests in 1987 forced free presidential elections after decades of military government. The 1988 Seoul Olympics announced a recovered, modern, democratic Korea to the world. Today South Korea is the world's 13th-largest economy and a major global cultural force: K-pop, K-drama, Korean cinema (Parasite, four Academy Awards in 2020), Korean food and beauty culture. BTS is among the world's highest-earning musical acts. Samsung's revenue exceeds the GDP of Portugal.

The Contemporary Relationship with Japan
The 1965 treaty normalising relations included $800 million in grants and loans -- described as economic cooperation, not reparations, a distinction that remains legally and emotionally significant. Japan has issued multiple apologies (the 1995 Murayama Statement most significantly) but nationalist politicians have repeatedly undermined them. The comfort women statue outside the Japanese embassy in Seoul, which Japan demands Korea remove, remains a daily flashpoint. Visiting both countries in the same trip, understanding this history, makes both richer and more complex experiences.

Japan & Korea · What to Eat, Drink & Know
Introduction · Japanese Food Philosophy
Japanese cuisine is built on principles so different from Western cooking that understanding them transforms what you taste. The foundation is not richness, spice or bold flavour — it is restraint, precision and seasonality. A great Japanese chef's highest ambition is to let an ingredient express itself perfectly at the exact moment it is at its peak. The concept of shun (旬) — eating with the seasons — governs every serious Japanese kitchen. In July, this means ayu (sweetwater fish), young ginger, edamame at its brief peak, fresh corn in Hokkaido, hamo (pike conger eel) in Kyoto, and the first of the season's sea urchin on the Shakotan Peninsula. You are arriving at one of the most delicious moments of the Japanese food year. The invisible backbone of almost everything is dashi — a stock made from kombu (dried kelp) and katsuobushi (dried, fermented, shaved bonito) that is the purest expression of umami. Unlike Western stocks which are cooked for hours, dashi is made in minutes. Its flavour is subtle, almost imperceptible — but remove it and everything tastes flat. You will rarely see dashi listed on a menu, but it is in the soup, the sauce, the simmered vegetables, the dipping liquid for the tempura. Learning to taste it is one of the pleasures of eating seriously in Japan. Umami itself — the fifth taste alongside sweet, sour, salty and bitter — was identified and named by Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda in 1908, who isolated it from kombu. Japan has the highest concentration of umami-rich ingredients in the world: kombu, katsuobushi, miso, soy sauce, sake, mirin, shiitake mushrooms. This is why Japanese food tastes deeply satisfying without being heavy. The visual presentation of Japanese food is never decoration for its own sake. The principle of ma (間) — negative space — means a single piece of sashimi on a large plate is not sparse, it is considered. The five colours (red, white, yellow, green, black) and five cooking methods (raw, simmered, grilled, steamed, fried) traditionally appear in a complete meal, balancing nutrition, texture and visual harmony. The ceramic or lacquerware chosen for each dish is integral to the experience — at a serious kaiseki restaurant, the tableware is selected seasonally and is often centuries old. One final principle: omotenashi — the Japanese concept of hospitality without expectation of return. There is no tipping in Japan, ever. The exceptional service you will experience is not motivated by gratuity but by a genuine desire to make your experience perfect. Attempting to tip can cause embarrassment. Simply expressing genuine appreciation — a bow, a sincere arigatou gozaimashita — is the correct response.
The Building Blocks · Dashi, Soy & Fermentation
Dashi is made fresh daily in every serious Japanese kitchen. Kombu dashi (just kelp, no bonito) has a clean, oceanic depth. Awase dashi (kombu and katsuobushi together) is the most common — the foundation of miso soup, ramen broth, soba tsuyu, tempura dipping sauce and the simmering liquid for almost every vegetable dish. Some restaurants, like Kiyama in Tokyo's Kagurazaka, build their entire philosophy around the quality of their dashi water. Soy sauce (shoyu) is not one thing — there are six main types. Koikuchi (dark, standard) is what you know. Usukuchi (light coloured but saltier, used in Kyoto cooking to preserve the colour of vegetables). Tamari (thick, rich, almost no wheat, the one closest to the original Chinese soy). White shoyu (shiro) — barely coloured, delicate, used in Kyoto kaiseki. Each region has its preferences. In Kyoto you will notice the food is lighter coloured than Tokyo — this is the usukuchi at work. Miso varies as dramatically as wine. Shiro (white) miso from Kyoto is sweet and mild, fermented only weeks. Aka (red) miso from Nagoya is deeply savory and fermented for years. Awase miso blends the two. Hokkaido uses a distinctively rich miso in its famous soup curry and ramen. Each region's miso soup will taste different — paying attention to this is one of the quiet pleasures of moving through Japan. Fermentation is the thread running through Japanese food culture: miso, soy, sake, mirin, rice vinegar, tsukemono (pickles), katsuobushi (fermented bonito), natto (fermented soybeans — an acquired taste). Japan has one of the most sophisticated fermentation traditions in the world, predating European cheesemaking by centuries.
Restaurant Types · What You Will Encounter
Kaiseki Ryori 懐石料理
The pinnacle of Japanese cuisine — a multi-course seasonal meal with a defined structure rooted in the Zen tea ceremony. Courses follow a set sequence: sakizuke (amuse-bouche), hassun (seasonal platter), mukozuke (sashimi), takiawase (simmered dish), yakimono (grilled dish), rice, miso and pickles, plus wagashi (sweet) with matcha. The pleasure is cumulative and intellectual as much as sensory — flavours are deliberately quiet, building on each other across two to three hours. You will experience this at Kikunoi Honten in Kyoto. Approach it with patience and curiosity rather than expecting bold flavours.
Kappo 割烹
Counter dining in front of the chef — less formal than kaiseki but still serious. The chef prepares dishes to order and often interacts with guests throughout the meal. Den in Tokyo operates in this spirit — intimate, chef-led, responsive. The best kappo meals feel like a conversation between chef and guest.
Izakaya 居酒屋
The Japanese pub — and the setting for some of the best casual eating of the trip. You order multiple small dishes to share over drinks, arriving as they're ready rather than in courses. Standard izakaya dishes: edamame, karaage (fried chicken), yakitori (skewered chicken), dashimaki tamago (rolled omelette), potato salad (oddly addictive), sashimi, fried tofu, grilled fish. Beer, sake, shochu and highballs (whisky soda) are the drinks. The etiquette: pour for your companions before yourself, never pour your own drink. Say "kanpai" before the first sip. Don't begin eating until everyone has a drink.
Ramen ラーメン
Japan's great regional dish — and the regional differences are profound. Sapporo miso ramen (Hokkaido): corn, butter, thick wavy noodles, rich miso broth — the style that fits the cold climate. This is what you'll find in Hokkaido and it is exceptional. Tokyo shoyu ramen: clear soy-based chicken broth, thin straight noodles, lighter. Hakata tonkotsu (Fukuoka): white pork bone broth, boiled for 18+ hours until opaque, very rich, thin noodles. Kyoto ramen: chicken and pork broth with thick noodles, hearty. Ramen shops are often tiny (8–12 seats), cash only, and may have a vending machine for tickets at the door — order your ramen, pay the machine, hand the ticket to the cook. Slurping is correct and expected — it cools the noodles and is considered an expression of enjoyment.
Sushi 寿司
The two main formats: omakase (chef-chosen nigiri served one or two pieces at a time at a counter — the serious version) and kaiten (conveyor belt, casual and fun). For nigiri, eat it in one or two bites — it is designed to be consumed the moment it is placed in front of you. It's acceptable to eat nigiri with your fingers. Dip fish-side down into soy sauce, never rice-side (the rice absorbs too much and falls apart). Ginger (gari) is a palate cleanser between different fish, not a condiment to eat with the sushi. The progression at a serious omakase typically moves from light white fish through oilier fish to fatty tuna, shellfish and finally egg (tamago) — following the flavour arc.
Yakiniku 焼肉
Japanese BBQ — you grill cuts of wagyu at your table over charcoal or gas. Unlike Korean BBQ (where the meat is often marinated), Japanese yakiniku focuses on the purity of the beef, with minimal seasoning — salt, a touch of sesame oil, a dipping sauce (tare) of soy, mirin and sake. The best yakiniku restaurants, like Namaiki (your booking on 7 July), operate as omakase — the staff grill each cut for you to the exact right temperature. Wagyu grading: A5 is the highest Japanese grade, with extraordinary marbling. Because of the fat content, portions are small — a few thin slices per course rather than a steak. This is not a failing, it is intentional.
Tempura 天ぷら
Battered and deep-fried seafood and vegetables — but nothing like Western batter. The tempura batter is extraordinarily light, almost translucent, applied cold and fried at precise temperatures. A proper tempura restaurant (tenpura-ya) serves each piece individually, the moment it comes out of the oil, and you eat it immediately. The dipping sauce is tentsuyu — dashi, mirin and soy — with grated daikon and ginger. At a serious tempura counter, the chef chooses the order of ingredients to tell a flavour story. July ingredients: anago (conger eel), corn, shiso leaf, kabocha pumpkin.
Yakitori 焼き鳥
Skewered grilled chicken — every part of the bird, nothing wasted. The best yakitori restaurants source their chicken from specific farms and use every cut: momo (thigh), negima (thigh and spring onion), tsukune (chicken meatball), kawa (skin, grilled till crispy), reba (liver), hatsu (heart), sunagimo (gizzard). Two seasonings: shio (salt — use for lighter cuts where you want to taste the chicken) and tare (the restaurant's house sauce — a reduction of soy, mirin and sake built up over years by adding to the same pot). Start with shio, finish with tare. Order one or two at a time rather than all at once.
Tonkatsu とんかつ
Breaded pork cutlet — Japan's great underrated comfort food. The best tonkatsu uses premium kurobuta (Berkshire) pork, panko breadcrumbs, and is fried in clean oil at precise temperature. Served with shredded cabbage, rice, miso soup, pickles and a thick Worcestershire-style sauce. Slice with the provided tongs or scissors before eating. A good tonkatsu lunch costs ¥1,500–2,500 and is one of the most satisfying meals in Japan.
Okonomiyaki お好み焼き
Two completely different dishes sharing a name. Osaka style: all ingredients (cabbage, pork, seafood, egg) mixed into a thick batter and cooked as one pancake — thick, hearty, topped with okonomiyaki sauce, mayonnaise, bonito flakes and aonori (dried seaweed). Hiroshima style (which you will eat on 20 July at Okonomimura): layered rather than mixed — a thin crepe base, then mountains of shredded cabbage, then bean sprouts, pork belly, then soba or udon noodles, then egg cracked and spread, then flipped and pressed. Larger, more complex, arguably more delicious. The chef watches the bonito flakes wave from the heat while applying the finishing sauce — this is the moment to photograph. Budget ¥1,000–1,500.
Donburi 丼
Rice bowl dishes — fast, delicious, affordable. Gyudon: thin-sliced beef simmered in sweet soy, on rice — Yoshinoya is the chain, but a good gyudon-ya does it far better. Katsudon: tonkatsu and egg simmered in dashi on rice. Tendon: tempura on rice with tare sauce. Kaisendon: fresh sashimi on rice — what you'll find in Hokkaido and Miyakojima at its absolute best. Unadon: grilled eel (unagi) lacquered with sweet soy on rice — July is peak unagi season in Japan. One of the great seasonal eating experiences.
Shabu Shabu & Sukiyaki しゃぶしゃぶ・すき焼き
Hot pot dining. Shabu shabu: thin wagyu sliced paper-thin, swished (the name is onomatopoeic) for 2–3 seconds in a light kombu broth, dipped in ponzu (citrus soy) or sesame sauce. The purity of the beef is the point. Sukiyaki: richer, sweeter — wagyu cooked in a cast iron pan with soy, sugar and mirin, then dipped in raw egg. Divisive but extraordinary with good beef. Namaiki serves some of their cuts sukiyaki style.
Konbini コンビニ
Do not underestimate the Japanese convenience store — 7-Eleven, FamilyMart and Lawson in Japan are a genuinely extraordinary food destination. The onigiri (rice balls) are made fresh twice daily, the sandwiches are better than most cafés, the hot foods (fried chicken, nikuman steamed pork buns) are excellent, the ice creams are exceptional (look for Haagen-Dazs green tea or the seasonal Hokkaido milk bars in Hokkaido specifically). Coffee from the machine is surprisingly good. Open 24 hours. Budget ¥500–800 for a complete breakfast. An essential part of the Japan experience, not a compromise.
Drinking in Japan
Sake 日本酒
Brewed from rice, water, koji mould and yeast — not distilled. The key grades: Junmai (pure rice, no added alcohol, more savoury and full-bodied). Ginjo (rice polished to 60% remaining, more fragrant, lighter). Daiginjo (polished to 50%, the most delicate and aromatic). Nigori (unfiltered, cloudy, slightly sweet — an excellent introduction). Temperature: premium sake is served chilled or at room temperature (not heated, which is for cheaper sake in winter). To order: "omakase de onegaishimasu" (please choose for me) at a good sake bar works perfectly — the sommelier will match the sake to your food. July in Kyoto means summer sake (natsu-shu) — lighter, slightly acidic, designed to be refreshing in the heat.
Shochu 焼酎
Distilled (unlike sake which is brewed), made from sweet potato, barley, rice or buckwheat. Popular in Kyushu and Okinawa. In Miyakojima you will encounter awamori — Okinawa's indigenous spirit, distilled from Thai indica rice and aged in clay pots. Older awamori (kusu) develops extraordinary depth. Try it on the rocks (on za rokku) with a water chaser. Significantly stronger than sake (25–40% ABV). A bottle from Miyakojima makes a meaningful souvenir.
Japanese Whisky ウイスキー
Some of the finest whisky in the world is now Japanese — Nikka, Suntory (Yamazaki, Hakushu, Hibiki), Mars Shinshu. The Highball (whisky and soda, usually Suntory Toki, served in a tall glass with one large ice cube and a thin lemon twist) is one of the great Japanese drinks — refreshing, easy, pairs with almost any food. Whisky bars in Tokyo and Kyoto carry extraordinary back catalogues at prices well below auction. Golden Gai in Shinjuku has several serious whisky bars.
Beer & Non-Alcoholic ビール
The major Japanese beers (Asahi Super Dry, Kirin Ichiban, Sapporo) are clean, crisp and highly drinkable — particularly Sapporo, which you should drink on draught in Hokkaido where it is made. The craft beer scene has exploded — look for Yo-Ho Brewing (Yona Yona Ale), Baird Beer, and numerous Tokyo craft taprooms. Non-alcoholic: Mugicha (barley tea, served chilled in summer — ubiquitous and delicious), Canned coffee from vending machines (hot or cold — a uniquely Japanese experience), Ramune (lemon-lime soda in the distinctive marble-stopper bottle — a summer festival staple).
Regional Food · What to Eat Where
🇰🇷 Seoul · 2–5 July
Korean food is bolder than Japanese — fermented, spicy, communal. Every meal comes with banchan (small shared side dishes — kimchi, spinach, bean sprouts, pickled radish) that are refilled for free. You eat from shared plates rather than individual portions.

Must eat in Seoul: Samgyetang (whole chicken stuffed with ginseng and rice in broth — the July heat remedy, try at Tosokchon) · Hanwoo galbi at Majang Market (premium Korean beef grilled at the table, 20–30% cheaper than restaurants) · Bindaetteok and yukhoe (raw beef) at Gwangjang Market · Bibim naengmyeon (cold buckwheat noodles in gochujang — essential in July heat) at Woo Lae Oak · Sundubu jjigae (silken tofu stew, arrives bubbling) · Makgeolli (unfiltered rice wine) and haemul pajeon (seafood pancake) in Ikseon-dong · Dolsot bibimbap (in a hot stone pot, bottom rice crisps to nurungji) · Mapo galmaegi (pork skirt steak near Mangwon Market).

⚠️ Nut allergy note: Korean food is generally safe for tree nut and peanut allergies. Pine nuts appear occasionally in some royal court dishes and salads — worth asking. Sesame (fine for you) is everywhere. The greatest risk is in fusion restaurants or desserts — stick to traditional Korean food and you'll be fine.
🗼 Tokyo · 5–8 July & 21–23 July
Tokyo has more Michelin stars than any city on earth — but its greatest food is often the most casual. Eat at a standing ramen counter at midnight. Have sushi at Tsukiji Outer Market at 7am. Buy a convenience store onigiri and eat it on the Sumida River bank.

Must eat in Tokyo: Tsukiji Outer Market breakfast (fresh tuna sashimi, tamagoyaki, oysters at dawn) · Ramen — Tokyo shoyu style, clear chicken-soy broth, thin noodles · Yakitori in any smoky alley near Omoide Yokocho · Tonkatsu lunch — Katsu Shinjuku or similar, kurobuta pork, ¥1,800 · Wagyu yakiniku at Namaiki (7 July, confirmed) · Kaiseki-inspired creative Japanese at Den (21 July, confirmed) · Unagi (grilled eel) over rice — July is peak season · Konbini culture — onigiri, katsu sando, Hokkaido ice cream · Golden Gai bar hopping with whisky and small plates.

⚠️ Nut allergy note: Traditional Japanese food is very safe. Be careful with: desserts and ice cream toppings (walnut common), some pastries in café culture, anything with a Chinese influence. At Namaiki and Den — inform staff at the start of the meal, both restaurants will accommodate without issue.
🦑 Hokkaido · 8–11 July
Hokkaido is Japan's food island — extraordinary dairy, cold-water seafood, corn, potatoes, lamb and the finest sea urchin (uni) in the world. The fishing ban on Shakotan uni lifts in June, meaning July is peak season when you arrive.

Must eat in Hokkaido: Uni don at Rinkousou in Shakotan (fresh sea urchin over warm rice — possibly the best version of this dish in Japan) · Sapporo miso ramen (rich miso broth, corn, butter, thick wavy noodles — the definitive Hokkaido bowl) · Hokkaido soft serve ice cream (the milk fat content is higher than anywhere in Japan — even a motorway service station version is extraordinary) · Fresh crab (kegani — horsehair crab, and kani — snow crab) · Soup curry (a Sapporo invention — thin, aromatic curry broth with enormous separately-fried vegetables and meat) · Jingisukan (Genghis Khan) — grilled lamb on a dome-shaped iron plate, a Hokkaido specialty · Otaru sushi (counter sushi using Hokkaido seafood — stop here for lunch on 10 July on the drive to Chitose).

⚠️ Nut allergy note: Very safe. Hokkaido desserts occasionally use walnut — check kakigori (shaved ice) toppings and parfait menus. The dairy-heavy soft serves and ice creams are nut-free.
🏖️ Miyakojima · 11–14 July
Okinawan food is genuinely distinct from mainland Japanese cuisine — Ryukyuan royal court influences, Chinese techniques, tropical ingredients and a philosophy of longevity eating. The island of Miyakojima is famous for its seafood, its awamori, and its relaxed eating culture.

Must eat in Miyakojima: Soki soba (Okinawan noodle soup — soft braised pork rib in a light bonito and kombu broth, flat noodles made from wheat not buckwheat — nothing like Japanese soba) · Goya champuru (bitter melon stir-fry with tofu, egg and spam — sounds strange, tastes extraordinary) · Fresh grilled fish — the coral reef seafood around Miyakojima is exceptional, any waterfront restaurant · Kaisendon (fresh sashimi rice bowl, Miyakojima fish including grouper and snapper) · 燦伊良部島店 on Irabu Island (4.7 stars, ¥1,000–2,000, outstanding local food) · Awamori — try kusu (aged awamori) at a local bar, on ice with water · Shaved ice (kakigori) with tropical fruit syrups — essential in the July heat.

⚠️ Nut allergy — IMPORTANT: Jimami tofu (じーまーみ豆腐) is a Miyakojima/Okinawan speciality — it looks like tofu but is made entirely from peanuts. It may be served as a starter or included in set menus without being obvious. Always ask before eating anything described as tofu in Okinawa. It is delicious but will cause you problems. Say: "Jimami tofu wa pinatsu ga haitte imasu ka?" (じーまーみ豆腐はピーナッツが入っていますか？) — "Does this contain peanuts?"
⛩️ Kansai · 14–19 July
Kansai contains three completely different food cultures — Osaka (kuidaore, "eat till you drop"), Kyoto (refined, restrained, the most sophisticated cuisine in Japan) and Koyasan (shojin ryori, Buddhist vegetarian). Moving between them in five days is a masterclass in Japanese food's range.

Osaka must eat: Takoyaki (octopus balls — Osaka's street food pride, crispy outside, molten inside, bonito flakes waving on top, ¥500 for 8) · Kushikatsu (skewered and breaded fried everything — meat, vegetables, cheese, dipped once only in communal sauce, "no double dipping" is the law) at Shinsekai · Okonomiyaki Osaka-style · Dotonbori street food evening · Nara: if stopping en route, try kakinoha-zushi (pressed sushi wrapped in persimmon leaf).

Kyoto must eat: Summer kaiseki at Kikunoi Honten (17 July, confirmed — ayu sweetfish, young ginger, heirloom vegetables) · Obanzai (Kyoto's everyday home cooking — small vegetable and tofu dishes served at lunch, try any obanzai restaurant near Nishiki Market) · Nishiki Market browsing — tsukemono (pickled vegetables, try the ones on sticks), fresh yudofu (tofu simmered in kombu water), dashimaki tamago · Matcha everything — soft serve, warabi mochi, parfait · Pontocho restaurant with kawadoko (riverside terrace dining over the Kamo River in summer) · Yatsuhashi — the Kyoto souvenir, cinnamon rice cake wafer (both baked crisp and soft raw versions).

Koyasan: Shojin ryori (Buddhist vegetarian kaiseki) at Fudo-in — tofu, seasonal vegetables, sesame, mountain vegetables prepared with extraordinary care. No meat, no fish, no strong-smelling vegetables (onion, garlic). Austere but beautiful. Portions are light — bring snacks for Maya.

⚠️ Nut allergy note: Shojin ryori at Koyasan uses sesame extensively (fine for you). Kyoto wagashi (sweets) occasionally contain walnut — ask before trying unfamiliar confections at Nishiki Market. Kikunoi and Kiyama kaiseki restaurants will accommodate your allergy if informed at the start of the meal.
🕊️ Hiroshima · 19–21 July
Hiroshima has two great food identities: the oyster (kaki) and the okonomiyaki. Both are extraordinary.

Must eat in Hiroshima: Hiroshimayaki at Okonomimura (20 July evening — layered, complex, fundamentally different from Osaka style) · Grilled oysters at waterfront stalls (¥250 each, grilled over charcoal right in front of you — the best way to eat them) · Oyster ramen (a local innovation — oyster-enriched tonkotsu broth) · Momiji manju from Miyajima (maple leaf-shaped cakes filled with red bean paste, matcha cream, or chocolate) · Anago meshi (conger eel over rice, a Miyajima speciality entirely different from freshwater unagi — lighter, less fatty) at a restaurant near the ferry terminal.

⚠️ Nut allergy note: Momiji manju also comes in a walnut filling — avoid this version, stick to red bean (azuki), matcha or chocolate. Ask "kore wa kurumi desu ka?" (これはくるみですか？) — "Is this walnut?" Oysters and okonomiyaki are completely nut-free.
Practical Notes · How to Eat in Japan
How to find a restaurant: Look for the noren — the short fabric curtain hanging in the doorway. If it's up, they're open. If it's inside, they're closed. Plastic food displays in the window show exactly what you'll get and the price. A queue outside a tiny restaurant is always a good sign — Japanese people queue for quality, not novelty. Don't be intimidated by restaurants where you can't see a menu in English — point at the plastic display, hold up two fingers, bow. It almost always works. Ordering: Say "sumimasen" (excuse me) to get a waiter's attention — never wave or shout. Water and green tea are free and refilled without asking. Many restaurants have a small "otoshi" or "tsukidashi" charge (¥300–500pp) — a small snack brought automatically, similar to a cover charge. This is normal. Tipping: Never. Not in restaurants, not in taxis, not at hotels. It can cause genuine confusion and mild offense. The price on the menu is what you pay, plus 10% consumption tax. Paying: Many traditional Japanese restaurants are cash only (genkin nomi) — always carry yen. Major tourist restaurants and chain restaurants accept cards. Convenience stores accept IC cards (Suica). When paying, place money on the small tray provided rather than handing it directly to the cashier. Useful phrases:
"Itadakimasu" — said before eating (roughly "I humbly receive"), always
"Gochisosama deshita" — said after eating ("what a feast"), always
"Oishii" — delicious
"Okaikei onegaishimasu" — the bill please
"Omakase de onegaishimasu" — please choose for me
"Kore wa nan desu ka?" — what is this?
"Mizu onegaishimasu" — water please

The Things You Might Miss
Ekiben (駅弁) — station bento boxes. Every major Japanese station has its own regional bento, made fresh, sold on platforms and in station halls. They range from ¥800 to ¥3,000 and are taken extremely seriously — some have cult followings. Buy one at Kyoto Station before boarding the Shinkansen to Hiroshima on 19 July. The experience of opening a beautiful lacquer box on a bullet train moving at 300km/h is uniquely Japanese. Depachika (デパ地下) — department store basement food halls. The basement level of any major Japanese department store is one of the great food shopping experiences in the world. Fresh sashimi, wagashi (traditional sweets), artisan pickles, high-end bento, imported cheese and charcuterie, extraordinary pastry. Isetan Shinjuku has perhaps the finest. Allow 45 minutes and bring cash. Morning markets — Tsukiji Outer Market in Tokyo (go before 8am for the freshest tuna sashimi and tamagoyaki), Nishiki Market in Kyoto (opens 9am), Otaru morning stalls on the canal (if passing through on 10 July). Vending machines (自動販売機) — Japan has 5 million of them, roughly one per 25 people. They sell hot and cold canned coffee, green tea, sports drinks, sometimes hot soup, occasionally hot ramen. The Pokka hot corn soup can in winter is a national institution. In July, look for cold Pocari Sweat (the best sports rehydration drink for hot weather) and Mitsuya Cider. July seasonal specials: Kakigori (shaved ice with flavoured syrup — matcha, yuzu, strawberry), unadon (grilled eel, peak season July–August), ayu (sweetwater river fish, grilled on skewers, intensely seasonal), edamame (fresh green soybeans at their brief summer peak), cold soba.
